I presented myself at the vault and demanded my initiation into the grade of Adeptus Minor. The London adepts refused. Their grounds were various and, to my mind, frivolous: that my moral character was unsuitable, that my sponsor Mathers had acted without authority, that the Second Order had voted against my advancement. What they could not say — what not one of them had the courage to say openly — was that they feared what I carried in me.

Yeats was among them. He had written to Mathers in terms that I thought unworthy of a poet; terms that were, in substance, political. He spoke of the Order's reputation, of the embarrassment my presence caused. He did not speak of magic. He had already ceased to practise it in any serious sense, and had settled instead for the manufacture of symbols, for the aesthetics of the occult without its substance.

I went to Paris. Mathers received me, initiated me, and sent me back to London with his written authority to take possession of the Order's rooms and papers. What followed was civil war. The London members locked me out. They changed the seals. They expelled Mathers — their own Chief — by democratic vote, as though the Great Work were a matter of parliamentary procedure.

I have never recovered my respect for committee decisions in matters of initiation. Either a man has received the Light or he has not. No ballot determines it.
