Willie talked to me of his magical work as though it were continuous with his poems — as though the two were one effort, conducted by different means. I did not think they were separate things either, but I thought he did not yet understand what the work demanded. He was still trying to have his vision and his comfort both. You cannot do that. Ireland did not want a poet who visited her in ecstasy and then went back to his supper. She wanted servants who stayed.

I had seen things in those years that I could not name in ordinary language — not metaphors, but actual things, forms in fire and light, presences that moved in rooms. Willie believed me; he was too honest not to. But he was also afraid, in a way that I was not, of what those presences asked. They asked everything. This is always the condition of the real vision: it wants you completely, or it leaves you entirely.

The Ireland I was serving was not a political idea. She was a living being, ancient and wounded and full of rage, and when I saw her — and I did see her — she was always a woman in a terrible state of grief who was also completely certain of what she wanted. What she wanted was not poetry. What she wanted was the blood and the fact.

Willie could not follow me there. He wrote poems about it, which is not the same thing, though the poems were beautiful, and I kept them.
