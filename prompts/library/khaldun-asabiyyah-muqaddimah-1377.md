Royal authority and dynasties are attained through groups and asabiyyah. Group feeling produces the ability to defend oneself, to press one's claims, and, if God wills it, to gain supreme control. Anyone who loses his group feeling is too weak to accomplish any of these things.

The nature of luxury is this: it corrupts tribes in the same way that it corrupts individuals. When a tribe has won power and its members have grown accustomed to ease and plenty, the younger generations no longer know what the struggle cost. They know its fruits but not its roots. They become quarrelsome over small things, which is the sign that the real thing — the asabiyyah, the sense of mutual obligation — has become hollow at the centre. The outward forms remain. The inner substance is gone.

I have studied the dynasties of the Maghrib, of Egypt, of the East, and they all follow the same course. The first generation wins. The second consolidates. The third lives on the memory. The fourth does not even trouble to remember. By the fifth, the dynasty is ripe for conquest by some new group from the desert or the margin, a group that is still hard, still bound together by necessity, still possessed of the original asabiyyah. And so the wheel turns.

I do not say this to despair. I say it because it is the nature of the world, and the nature of the world is a subject of which God approves the study. The physician who knows the course of a disease is not made cruel by that knowledge. He is made useful.
